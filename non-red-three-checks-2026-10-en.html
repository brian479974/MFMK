NON-RED · THREE CHECKS

NEWS · THE LAST TWO MONTHS

Three companies, three prosecutors’ offices, one way of being caught

Between August and October, at least three companies came under investigation by prosecutors in Qiaotou, Taoyuan and Taichung for fitting mainland-made chips, flight-control boards or cameras into drones delivered to Taiwan’s armed forces. The heads of two of them have been detained (United Daily News, 29 October). These are allegations under investigation and have not been ruled on by a court.

Policy has not softened. In January the Vice President said that government drone production and procurement, for the military and the public sector alike, must reach a 100% non-red supply chain (China Times). With budgets, orders and policy all moving forward, scrutiny will only tighten.

What the industry should take from these cases is not who broke the rules, but where in the chain the problem was found.

HISTORY · REMOVING COSTS MORE THAN CHECKING

Ripping out Huawei cost the US 2.6 times the original budget

Section 889 of the US National Defense Authorization Act for fiscal 2019 named Huawei and ZTE. A 2020 law then paid telecom carriers to tear the two companies’ equipment out of networks already in service and replace it — the programme known as rip and replace.

The lesson is about sequence, not the amount. Checking origin before equipment goes in costs procurement time. Finding out after it is installed and running costs removal, redesign, downtime and a full re-qualification.

Taiwan’s drone industry is still at the stage before the equipment goes in. This is when getting the checks right costs least.

FRAMEWORK · THREE CHECKS

Non-red has three checks, and the third is the one that slips

Lay these cases side by side and non-red turns out to be three checks, each harder than the last.

THE THREE CHECKS OF NON-RED
1
On paper

Passed in every case here

In the teardown

Where these cases were caught
3
After the revision
New design, new source, end-of-life part — prove it all again
Most schedule pressure, least oversight
For any non-red dispute in the news, ask first: which check did it fail?

The first check is paper. Certificates of origin, contract clauses, supplier declarations. It stops mistakes, not deliberate fakes. In these cases the paperwork had been filed.

The second check is the hardware. Open the box and the markings on a chip do not lie. But teardowns are sampled, and they only cover the batch being delivered.

The third check is time. A drone is not a frozen product; it is a platform that changes every quarter. A longer arm, a different motor, a part going end-of-life — every change brings in new parts and new suppliers, and the first two checks start over. That is also the moment of greatest schedule pressure and the strongest temptation to cut corners.

MARKET · HOME AND ABROAD

At home a requirement, abroad still a bonus — both meet at the third check

Taiwan’s military contracts already make non-red a requirement, with criminal liability for breaches. The first two checks will only get tighter.

Exports look different. Czechia became the largest market for Taiwanese drones in the first quarter, yet CzechInvest says European procurement still treats non-red as a preference rather than a rule; on the Ukrainian front line, buyers still look at volume, price and lead time (TechNews). The same report quotes a Ukrainian National Guard liaison officer with one example:

Both kinds of buyer end up at the third check. The military wants a drone that is still non-red a year and three revisions later; overseas integrators want revisions that keep pace with the battlefield. Only a supplier that can prove origin after every revision, without re-running supplier qualification for each new part, passes both.

CASE · ONE DISCONTINUED PART

Find a new supplier, or make it on site

At a US Army training base, a hatch plug on one of its vehicles had gone out of production. Reproducing it would have cost roughly US$10,000, with a three-month lead time — which means a new supplier, a new origin check and a new place in someone else’s queue.

The base printed it on site on Markforged printers in Onyx composite instead, for about US$230, and cut the design from ten parts to four; that application alone has saved US$244,000 (Markforged case study).

From a non-red point of view the saving is secondary. Instead of one more outside supplier and one more set of origin papers to verify, there is an on-site machine that has already been qualified and a material lot record. What the third check has to re-verify shrinks from an unfamiliar new vendor to a platform checked long ago.

WHERE · WHERE TO START

The three kinds of part that change most

For drone makers and tier-1 suppliers, the parts revised most often and outsourced at shortest notice fall into three groups. They share small volumes, frequent changes and urgent lead times — and they are where origin is hardest to pin down.

Images: Markforged

Keep these three in-house, on equipment and materials of simple, known origin, and far less has to be re-verified at the third check. Revision cycles shorten, and with them the temptation to cut corners under schedule pressure.

CONCLUSION · ONE THING TO DO NOW

Start with the last revision

Pick one drone in service and list every part number added or replaced in its most recent revision. For each one, confirm two things: whether its origin paperwork matches what you would find if you opened it up, and where the replacement would come from if it went out of production next month.

If a revision leaves you without fresh proof, you have only passed the first check.
