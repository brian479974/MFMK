Key takeaways

  • The three cases from August to October all involved electronics — chips, flight-control boards and cameras — and in every case the drones had already been delivered to the armed forces; one was caught only at the army’s acceptance teardown. Checks will only tighten, qualifying sources will shrink, and any one supplier’s problem can stop a production line.
  • 3D printing cannot make a chip. What it can bring in-house is the layer of airframe structures, fixtures and end-of-life spares: the parts that change most, are outsourced at shortest notice and have the least predictable lead times.
  • Armed forces already remake discontinued parts on base and turn two-to-three-week resupply into hours; in Taiwan, the ASTER-SWARM airframe is revised, repaired and produced entirely at home. The first step is to walk the production line once and identify the parts worth bringing in.

None of the three military drone cases referred to prosecutors in Taiwan over the past two months were stopped by a document check: all three companies’ drones had already been delivered to the armed forces. The contracts banned parts made in mainland China, and one company had even supplied false certificates of origin; in the Taichung case, the army opened the airframes at acceptance as a precaution, found chips marked “CN” and referred the matter to prosecutors. For the industry, that means every part will now have to survive a teardown, and fewer sources will qualify. The fewer the sources, the more easily one supplier going end-of-life, slipping a delivery or losing its qualification can stop your line.

PRODUCTION RESILIENCE

NEWS · THE LAST TWO MONTHS

Three companies, three prosecutors’ offices, and every part in question was electronic

Between August and October, at least three companies came under investigation by prosecutors in Qiaotou, Taoyuan and Taichung for fitting mainland-made chips, flight-control boards or cameras into drones delivered to Taiwan’s armed forces. A founder and a general manager at two of the companies have been detained (United Daily News, 29 October). These are allegations under investigation and have not been ruled on by a court.

Policy has not softened. In January the Vice President said that government drone production and procurement, for the military and the public sector alike, must reach a 100% non-red supply chain (China Times). With budgets, orders and policy all moving forward, scrutiny will only tighten.

Purchasing and production feel it first. Every supplier and every part number has to come with proof, and any that cannot must be replaced; a new supplier means new quotes, a new origin check and a new place in someone else’s queue, and the line waits in the meantime. So these cases raise a second question beyond compliance: when an outside source fails, which parts can you make in your own plant?

BOUNDARY · WHAT IT CANNOT DO

A drone has three layers of parts, and 3D printing can take on two of them

Start with the limits. The chips, flight-control boards and cameras at the centre of these cases cannot be 3D printed; resilience in the electronics layer comes from qualified suppliers, origin traceability and safety stock. Industrial 3D printing can take on the other two layers.

THE THREE LAYERS OF A DRONE
1
Electronics
Chips, flight controllers, cameras, radio modules
Not printable: rely on qualified suppliers and traceability
2
Structures
Arms, frames, brackets, guards, payload interfaces
Printable in-house: revise the file, add continuous carbon fibre for strength
3
Production
Fixtures, gauges, poka-yoke plates, end-of-life spares
Printable in-house: no fight for CNC time, no new source for discontinued parts
Every problem part in these cases sat in the electronics layer; the parts revised most and outsourced at shortest notice sit in the structural and production layers.

What the structural and production layers share is low volume, frequent change and urgent lead times. A longer arm, a different motor or a part going end-of-life means structures and fixtures have to change too; outsourced machining means new stock and a new slot in the schedule, and the wait is often longer than the design work itself.

At an executive roundtable during Land Forces 2026 in Australia this October, Markforged’s Vice President for Asia Pacific, Richard Elving, was measured about the scope: additive cannot print a whole platform; its place is in sustainment, producing critical components and temporary replacement parts, and for the manufacturers in the room it might cover one to five per cent of what they make (Markforged media hub report). The share is small, but when a line stops, these are often the parts it is waiting for.

SUSTAINMENT · DISCONTINUED AND DELAYED PARTS

Discontinued parts remade on base, and two-to-three-week resupply cut to hours

When a source fails, it usually fails in one of two ways. In the first, a part goes out of production and the original maker stops supplying it, so someone has to find a new supplier to tool or machine it. A US Army training base faced exactly this: a hatch plug on one of its vehicles was discontinued, and reproducing it would have cost about US$10,000 with a three-month lead time. The base printed it on site on Markforged printers in Onyx composite for about US$230, and cut the design from ten parts to four (Markforged case study).

In the second, the part exists but sits at the far end of a supply line. During the US–Korea CJLOTS 26 exercise, Marines reverse-engineered and printed a boat strainer in under half a day inside an X-FAB expeditionary shelter equipped with Markforged Mark Two printers, turning a two-to-three-week resupply into a few hours (DVIDS).

Both situations were solved the same way: the part’s source moved from an outside supplier to a qualified machine on site, a design file and a recorded lot of material. For a maker that has to stay non-red, that is also one fewer outside part number to trace.

REVISION · SPEED OF CHANGE

Overseas buyers want revisions faster than outsourced machining can deliver

Export markets care about something else. Czechia became the largest market for Taiwanese drones in the first quarter, yet CzechInvest says European procurement still treats non-red as a preference rather than a rule; on the Ukrainian front line, buyers still look at volume, price and lead time (TechNews). The same report quotes a Ukrainian National Guard liaison officer with one example:

A revision moves only as fast as its slowest part. If structures and fixtures depend on outside machining, every change joins the queue again; printed in-house, a revised design can become a part the same day. The armed forces want a drone that is still non-red a year and three revisions later, and overseas buyers want revisions that keep pace with the battlefield. Both need qualified parts soon after every revision.

CASE · AN EXAMPLE FROM TAIWAN

ASTER-SWARM: an FPV airframe that no longer depends on imported carbon plate

ASTER Technology’s ASTER-SWARM is a single-use FPV strike drone. An FPV airframe has to survive full-throttle acceleration, hard turns and dives within seconds, and frames printed in ordinary filament flex, resonate or even crack along the layer lines under that load. The usual alternative is a CNC-cut carbon-plate frame: strong enough, but dependent on imported carbon-fibre sheet and outside machining, with new stock and another wait for every design change (Markforged media hub case).

Two routes to the ASTER-SWARM airframe. Photo and test results provided by ASTER Technology, as published in the Markforged media hub case.

ASTER moved to printing every structural part of the airframe on a Markforged X7 Field Edition, with Onyx forming the shape and continuous carbon fibre laid along the load paths at the arm roots and motor mounts. The airframe is split into five standard modules — top plate, base frame, standoffs, front guard and payload interface — so damage means reprinting one part and a revision means updating one module’s file. Fitted with printed propeller guards, the same airframe becomes a trainer, and parts broken in training can be reprinted on site.

The prototype has completed take-off and high-speed flight tests and passed a Beaufort Force 9 wind test (mean wind speed 20.8–24.4 m/s), with the results measured and verified by a third party. Design, printing and assembly all take place in Taiwan, with no bought-in carbon-plate frames and no outside machining.

“An ordinary 3D-printed airframe shakes and cracks once it flies fast. With Markforged continuous carbon fiber printing, we could, for the first time, 3D print an airframe that truly holds up to high-speed flight. Add a modular design, and ASTER-SWARM can be revised, repaired and produced at scale entirely in Taiwan. That is exactly the capability self-reliant defense needs most.”

SECURITY · DESIGN DATA

Make the part on site, and keep the design file under your own control

Bringing parts in-house means planning data security at the same time: a leaked design file lets someone else make the same part. At Project Convergence 22, the British Army’s Royal Electrical and Mechanical Engineers (REME) received files for spares that US forces could not get through their own supply chains, printed them on site on mobile printers and handed the parts over; the only thing that crossed borders was the file (Mark3D video).

The files need protecting in transit too. Phase I of the US Army’s IBAS programme, worth US$7.5 million, is led by Automation Alley with NCDMM as prime contractor and Markforged and Fraunhofer USA as partners, and aims to prove that IP-protected manufacturing instructions can be streamed securely to distributed printers (PR Newswire).

FACTORY · THE FIRST STEP ON THE LINE

On the factory floor, the first batch is usually fixtures

Beyond the airframe, the production line has its own layer of parts that is easy to overlook. Drone makers and tier-1 suppliers change over often, and every changeover needs new fixtures, gauges and poka-yoke plates that compete with production parts for the same CNC time. After Yamaha Fine Technologies in Japan began making its production fixtures in-house on an X7, fixture lead time fell from about seven days to one, same day at best, and fixture cost fell to about 25% of what it had been; the X7 runs about 2,000 hours a year (Markforged customer case).

Fixtures never fly and never ship, so they carry the least risk and show results fastest, which makes them a sensible first batch. Once strength and dimensional data have built up, the same approach extends to structures and spares.

CONCLUSION · ONE THING TO DO NOW

List the ten parts that kept the line waiting longest last year

Pick one drone in service and list the ten parts that kept the line waiting longest over the past year. Mark each with its category, its outsourced lead time, how many times it was revised this year and whether the original maker still supplies it.

Leave the electronics to supply-chain management. Among structures, fixtures and spares, anything with an outsourced lead time measured in weeks, two or more revisions a year, or no original maker left belongs in the first batch to bring in-house.
